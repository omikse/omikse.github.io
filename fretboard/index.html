The same note, three ways

A dot on a string, a number on a tab stave, a blob on a staff. Guitar music is written an octave above where it sounds — that is what the little 8 under the clef means, and why the low E ends up three ledger lines below the staff rather than off the bottom of the page.

What one string is really doing

A string never vibrates at just one speed. It swings as a whole, and in halves, and in thirds, all at once — and those extra speeds are what make a guitar sound like a guitar instead of a test tone. Notice where they land: the octave, then a fifth above that, then another octave. The intervals musicians use were not invented. They were found inside a single string.

Four things worth knowing

An octave is a doubling. 110 Hz and 220 Hz are both A. Twelve frets up doubles the frequency, which is why the same shape sounds "the same but higher" and why the 12th fret has two dots.
One fret is always the same ratio, never the same distance. Each fret multiplies the frequency by 1.0595 (that is the twelfth root of 2). A ratio needs less and less string as you climb, which is exactly why the frets bunch up toward the body.
Sweet intervals are simple fractions. A fifth is nearly 3:2, a fourth nearly 4:3, an octave exactly 2:1. Play two of them and watch the waves lock into a repeating pattern. Play a tritone — 45:32 — and watch the pattern refuse to settle. That mess is the sound of the interval.
A capo is a movable nut. It does not change what your fingers do; it changes where zero is. Drag it to fret 2 and every shape you know sounds two semitones higher — the same song in a new key, with the same chords under your hand.

How to actually learn this

  • Start on one string. Six strings at once is six problems. Learn the low E, then the A — that is already every barre-chord and pentatonic root you need.
  • Naturals first. Seven names, and every sharp/flat is one fret from one of them. Turn on Naturals only until you're at 95%.
  • Use the anchors. Fret 5 and fret 7 give you the same note as the next string open (except G→B, which is fret 4). Fret 12 is the open string again.
  • Octave shapes beat counting. Two strings up and two frets across is the same note (one fret more once you cross to the B string). Drill it in Find every octave.
  • Short and daily. Five minutes every day beats an hour on Sunday. Watch the accuracy bars, not the score.

Changing chords in time

The hard part of a song is rarely a chord. It is arriving at the next one without the strumming hand stopping to wait. This keeps the bar going and tells you what is coming, so the change has to happen on the beat whether your fingers are ready or not. Slow it down until they are.

Getting it into your hand

  • Count out loud. “One and two and three and four and.” Not in your head — out loud. Rhythm that you cannot say, you cannot play.
  • Keep the hand moving on the misses. If your hand stops and restarts, the timing goes with it. Swing past the strings without touching them.
  • Slow enough to be boring. Drop the tempo until it is easy, then leave it there for a week. Speed is a side effect of accuracy, never the other way round.
  • One chord at first. Get the pattern automatic before you ask your other hand to change chords underneath it.

Why down and up are not interchangeable

A downstroke hits the low strings first and lands with the weight of your arm behind it; an upstroke catches the high strings first and arrives lighter. That difference is most of what makes a strum sound like a groove rather than a series of chords.

It also explains why patterns are written against a fixed hand motion. Play “down, down, up” by making three separate deliberate movements and it sounds stiff. Play the same three strikes while your hand is already swinging in eighths and it sits in the beat by itself.

A muted chuck is the same movement with the side of your palm resting on the strings. It makes no pitch, only a percussive click — which is exactly what a drummer would put there.

How to use this

  • Start with one box. Don't roam. Pick five frets, stay there, and let the chords move underneath you.
  • Play fewer notes than you want to. Two notes placed well beat twelve played fast. Leave silence between phrases — the loop keeps going without you.
  • Chase the rings. When the chord changes, aim for a note that just gained a blue ring. Landing on a chord tone is the difference between noodling over a progression and playing with it.
  • Sing it first. If you can hum the phrase, your hand will find it. If you can't, you are only moving fingers.

Why one scale covers the whole progression

Every chord in a key is built from the same seven notes, so a scale drawn from those notes cannot land on a wrong one. That is what "in a key" means, and it is why one pentatonic shape survives four chord changes.

The chords still differ, though, and that is what the rings are for. C and Am share six of seven notes but point at different homes. Play the same lick over both and the second one leans somewhere else. Following the rings is how you hear that rather than read it.

A twelve-bar blues cheats: its chords are all dominant sevenths, which do not strictly belong to one key at all. The minor pentatonic works over it anyway — that friction between a minor scale and major chords is the blues.

The five boxes, and why they connect

The pentatonic has five notes, so it has exactly five ways to sit under your hand — one starting on each note of the scale. Play them in order up the neck and the last one is the first one again, twelve frets higher. Nothing else is hiding.

Each box overlaps the next by two notes on every string. That overlap is the door: whenever you land on a shared note you can step into the neighbouring shape without stopping. Practise the joins, not the boxes.

Box numbers here follow the usual convention: box 1 is the one whose root sits on the low E string, played with the first finger.

Minor and major pentatonic are the same five notes

A minor pentatonic and C major pentatonic contain identical pitches — A C D E G. What changes is which note you treat as home. Land on A and it sounds mournful; land on C and it sounds bright. That is the whole trick behind “play the minor shape a fret-and-a-half down for the major sound”: the shape did not change, the target did.

Concretely: the major pentatonic of any key uses the minor pentatonic box three frets lower. C major pentatonic = A minor pentatonic, and A is three semitones below C.

Reading the circle

Step clockwise and you go up a fifth, adding one sharp each time; anticlockwise goes down a fifth (up a fourth) and adds a flat. Neighbouring keys differ by exactly one note, which is why moving one step in either direction sounds like a modulation rather than a car crash.

  • The three highlighted wedges are I, IV and V — the key's home and its two closest neighbours, the backbone of most songs you know.
  • The inner ring is the relative minor: same notes, same key signature, different home note.
  • Order of sharps: F C G D A E B. Flats are that list backwards: B E A D G C F.
  • To name a sharp key: the last sharp is one semitone below the tonic. For a flat key, the second-to-last flat is the tonic.

Why guitarists care

Fifths are how the guitar is tuned. Four of your five string intervals are perfect fourths — a fifth upside down. Move a shape one string set toward the bass and you have moved a fourth, which is one step anticlockwise on this circle. The circle is not an abstract diagram; it is the geometry of your own neck.

1 · The twelve notes

Western music uses twelve pitches, evenly spaced. On a guitar that is beautifully literal: one fret = one semitone, and twelve frets later you are back where you started, an octave higher.

Only two pairs of letters are a single fret apart — B→C and E→F. Every other letter pair has a fret between them, and that fret has two names (A♯ is B♭). Which name you use depends on the key, not on preference.

4 · Chords: stack every other note

Take a scale, start on a degree, skip one, take the next, skip one, take the next. Three notes is a triad; a fourth gives you a seventh chord. Do that on each degree of the scale and you get the chords that belong to the key — no other chords needed for most songs.

7 · The neck's own logic

Strings are fourths (with one lie)

E A D G B e. Every step is five frets — a perfect fourth — except G→B, which is four. That single exception is responsible for almost every "why does this shape change on the B string" question.

Practical rule: any shape that crosses from the G string to the B string shifts one fret higher.

Octave shapes

  • E→D string and A→G string: +2 strings, +2 frets.
  • D→B string and G→e string: +2 strings, +3 frets (the B-string shift again).
  • Any string to the next-but-two: +3 strings, −3 frets going up in pitch is the same note two octaves apart on E→e.

Learn one note on the low E and you get four more free.

Anchor frets

Fret 5 on any string equals the next string open — except the G string, where it's fret 4. Fret 12 is the octave: everything below repeats above. Frets 3, 5, 7, 9 and 12 have dots because they are the notes you reach for most.

CAGED in one paragraph

The open C, A, G, E and D chord shapes can each be moved up the neck. In that order they tile the whole fretboard for any one chord, and their five regions are exactly the five pentatonic boxes. Learn which shape a box belongs to and the scale stops being separate from the chord.

8 · A practice order that works

  1. Naturals on the low E and A strings — in the game, naturals only, strings 6 and 5, frets to 12. Get to 95%.
  2. Octave shapes — Find every octave mode. This is what stops you counting frets.
  3. All six strings, naturals, then unlock the sharps and flats.
  4. One pentatonic box, all twelve keys — box 1 only, move the root, say the note name out loud as you play it.
  5. Join two boxes, then three. Use the shared notes as the pivot.
  6. Name what you play. Improvise for a minute and name every note you land on. Slow, ugly, and the fastest thing on this list.
